What is netnography?

Understanding people through their online interactions. It is a qualitative method that studies how people communicate, behave, share opinions and make decisions in online communities.

Traditional researchNetnography
Researcher asks questionsResearcher observes conversations
Surveys and interviewsComments and online discussions
Responses are usually promptedInteractions occur naturally
Individual opinionsCommunity behaviour and culture
Offline or controlled settingDigital environment

In our topic, comment sections are informal product-research communities

Discover

Users see a product through Instagram, YouTube, TikTok or influencer content.

"Is it actually good?"

Question

They move to comments to ask about price, quality, link, size and real experience.

"Anyone tried this?"

Decide

They compare opinions, warnings and recommendations before buying.

"Any cheaper alternative?"

Why we use it

Understand behaviour. How users research products online.

Find needs. What information users actively seek.

Identify pain points. Where scrolling, noise and uncertainty occur.

Understand trust. What makes a comment feel credible.

Generate insights. Turn repeated patterns into user-centred requirements.

Three user types seen in the comments

The Researcher

"I will research before buying."

Needs: evidence, comparison, long-term use.

Pain: too much information.

The Social Shopper

"I saw it and want it now."

Needs: quick validation, price, link.

Pain: important info is scattered.

The Skeptic

"Can I trust the hype?"

Needs: independent experiences, negatives.

Pain: hard to separate hype from reality.

How it fits Design Thinking

Netnography feeds the human-centred design cycle: observe first, design second.

  1. EmpathizeObserve comments and behaviour
  2. DefineFrame the real information problem
  3. IdeateCategorise, summarise, compare
  4. PrototypeBuild the CommentCart interface
  5. TestCompare raw comments vs insights

Problem statement. Consumers struggle to extract trustworthy, useful product information from fragmented social-media conversations.

Core idea

Turn messy conversation into structured community knowledge, without losing the human experiences behind it. That is why the original comments stay visible next to every insight.

Benefits and honest limits

Benefits

  • Natural behaviour: what users really say and do
  • Authentic insight: spontaneous experiences
  • Community view: collective patterns, not isolated answers
  • Hidden needs: problems users never state directly
  • Trend awareness: changing concerns and preferences
  • Evidence-based design: observations tied to decisions

Limits, and what the app does about them

  • Data overload: groups comments into themes
  • Noise: spam and emoji-only comments are hidden from insights
  • Fake or promotional content: flagged, never counted as experience
  • Context loss: originals always shown beside the summary
  • Platform limits: you paste what you can see; nothing is scraped
  • Representation bias: comments are one slice of buyers, so treat verdicts as a signal

Conclusion. Social-media comments are more than reactions. They contain experiences, recommendations, warnings, questions and trust signals. The opportunity is not more reviews, it is making community knowledge usable.
